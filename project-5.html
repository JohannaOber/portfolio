Linköping University·Visualiseringscenter C·2024

LOVEsick

At 14, falling in love is confusing and embarrassing to ask about. We turned it into an exhibit where visitors examine a love-sick patient.

My role

Research·Scene development·Physical interaction concept·Technical integration

Topic

Exhibition·Interactive

Overview

01Problem

Making the science of love feel personal

The Visualiseringscenter C in Norrköping invites student teams to develop interactive science exhibits for young audiences. Our brief: explain the biology and psychology of falling in love in a way that is engaging, relatable and safe to explore, even for teenagers who might not admit they are curious.

02Approach

The emotional and sensory experience

Instead of explaining, we let visitors play: they become nurses and examine a love-struck patient. One body half shows the biology, the other the feelings, so visitors explore the topic without having to talk about themselves.

03Result

The museum’s favourite student project

An interactive exhibit where visitors examine a fictional patient with a tangible stethoscope, while a doctor’s voiceover guides them with warmth and humour. It received outstanding feedback from educators and visitors, and the Visualiseringscenter C selected it as their favourite student project, with potential for integration into the permanent exhibition.

Main decisions

01

Roleplay instead of explanation

As a nurse, visitors examine someone else instead of talking about their own feelings, which turns an embarrassing topic into something warm, funny and safe.

02

Two halves, free entry point

The left side answers ‘How does it work?’, the right side ‘What does it feel like?’. Visitors choose where to start, which creates curiosity without prescribing a single path.

03

A tangible stethoscope

A physical object to examine the patient with makes the interaction, and the topic, memorable.

Full process

We researched the biology and psychology of falling in love and studied how science museums engage young audiences. We mapped the emotions, questions, and knowledge gaps typical of first experiences with romantic feelings.

We defined the core narrative: a love-struck patient who needs examination. Two opposing perspectives shaped the exhibit's structure: the left side explores the biological and chemical mechanisms, the right the emotional and sensory experience. Visitors choose their entry point.

Visitors become nurses, using a physical tangible as a stethoscope to 'examine' the patient. A doctor narrates the findings, explaining that love is natural and nothing to worry about. The concept transforms a potentially embarrassing topic into something warm, funny, and safe.

The concept was prototyped in Watchout and evaluated for clarity, engagement, and educational impact. The dual-sided structure proved highly effective in creating curiosity and guiding exploration without prescribing a single path.
